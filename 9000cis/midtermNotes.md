## PP chapter summary

### Chapter 1
- General and functional managers (not just IT specialist) must be actively involved in information systems decisions. 
    - Manager is someone who is a knowledg worker who is in charge of a team, either for a functional area (functional managers) or an entire organization (general manager).
    - Managers must be ready to use technology.
- 4 groups: general managers, functional managers, end users, and IS professionals. First two cannot treat technology as someone else's problem.
- Chief Information Officer (CIO), technical staff, analysts, rise of data scientists and analytics roles. 

### Chapter 2
- Critical distinction between IT and IS. 
- 4 components of IS: IT, people, process, and structure.
- Organizational context shapes IS designs. Organizational change is first order (doing the same thing better) or second order (transforming how work is done).

### Chapter 3
- Surveys the landscape of systems that organizations actually use, providing a taxonomy and vocabulary for talking about them. 
- 3 perspectives for categorizing IS: hierarchical(operational, managerial, executive), functional(systems for finance, HR, marketing), and process-oriented(cross-functional workflows).
- Enterprise Resource Planning(ERP): integrating business processes across departments, with it major advantages (data consistency, efficiency) and limitations (cost, rigidity, implementation risk)
- Supply Chain Management(SCM) and Customer Relationship Management(CRM): coordinating upstream suppliers and downstream customer relationships respectively.
- Knowledge Management: Capturing and sharing organizational know-hows
- Business Intelligence(BI) and Big Data: the chapter traces the evolution from batch processing -> transaction systems -> big data era, explaining what each stage enabled
- Cloud Computing: three delivery models: SaaS, PaaS, and IaaS and how cloud has changed computing architecture from mainframes to client-server to the cloud.

### Chapter 4
- Explains how the internet fundamentally reshaped the economics of competition, equipping the managers with frameworks to understand and respond to these changes.
- 3 pillars:
    - Network Economics: how value in networks grows with the number of participants (network externalities), what causes markets to tip toward a dominant player, and the concept of positive feedback loops. eBay vs Amazon
    - Information Economics: the unique properties of information goods (near-zero maginal cost of reproduction, experience gods, public goods characteristics), and how information-intensive goods behave differently from physical products
    - Disruptive Technology: drawing from Christensen's framework, the chapter distinguishes between sustaining technologies (improving current products from mainstream customers) and disruptive technologies (starting in low-end or new markets and eventualling upending incumbents). 
- Process virtualization theory: accessing which business activities can be performed digitally without physical interaction.
- Richness vs reach trade-off

### Chapter 5
- A comprehensive overview of digital business
- Begins with foundational infrastrucutre: the Internet, Web 2.0, and the mobile platform.
- Then turns to business models, how digital innovation has enabled radical experimentation in how firms create and capture value. 
- Major revenue models like subscription, advertising, transaction fees, freemium, and categorizes digital business by transaction type (B2C, B2B, C2C, etc)
- Disintermediation(cutting out middlemen) and reintermediation(new digital middlemen emerging).
- Market efficiency gains, channel conflict, customer/employee self service, long-tail strategies (selling niche products at scale), and online-to-offline(O2O) commerce. 

### Chapter 7
- Chapter shifts focus to strategy, asking how do IS create economic value, and how can managers identify and evaluate strateic information systems?
- A rigorous framework centered on added value: the difference between total value created(customer willingness to pay minus supplier opportunity cost) and what competitors could offer.
- Managers distinguish tactical IS (operation improvements) and strategic IS (initiatives that alter compettive positioning)
- Central concept is IT-dependent strategic initiative: a strategic move that would be impossible or hard to do without the enabling IT. 

### Chapter 8
- Building on chapter 7, the chapter provides the practive analyrical toolkits managers use to identify opportunites and design IT-dependent strategic initiatives
- Frameworks covered includes:
    - Industry analysis (Porter's Five Forces): using IS to shift competitive dynamics.
    - Value chain analysis: pinpointing where in the firm's primary and support activities IS can reduce cost or increase differentiation
    - Customer Service Life Cycle(CSLC): mapping how IS can add value at each stage of the customer relationship (requirements, acquisition, ownership, retirement)
    - Virtual value chain: extending the physical value chain into the information space, where datat about activities can itself become a source of value
    - Customer data strategies: how firms can leverage data about customers to generate new revenue streams or deepen loyalty.
