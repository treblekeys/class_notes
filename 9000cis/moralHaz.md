**Executive Summary**
    This study looks at the moral hazard that is presented due to the buyback
contract scheme. It focuses on a supplier whose sales department encourages
retailers to over order inventory and retailers who are mostly protected
from the consequences of unsold stocks. This resulted in a accumulation of
excess inventory for the supplier. This led to the proposal of a
Vendor-Managed Inventory (VMI) system with a base stock policy to reduce
over ordering and provide accountability to inventory management. 

**Problem Analysis**
    This moral hazard in this supply chain is the flawed buyback contract that
separates financial risk from decision making. Retailers are able to return
unsold inventory to the supplier with the only consequence being that they are
required to purchase 30% in new goods. This means that retailers are encouraged
to overstock without any meaningful cost. Due to this, retailers do not have an
incentive to manage inventory, leading to overstocking. There is also the
matter with the supplier having no visibility into retailer's actual inventory
level or consumer demands. According to the article, suppliers are "unable to
access, monitor, or control retailers' inventories", which means they do not
have genuine information regarding market demand and status of retailer's
inventories (pg. 7). Without observable actions, moral hazard is due to arise. 
As such, there are operational consequences. After product Y and Z were
launched, supplier's inventory went from 300,000 units to 600,000 units within
months and obsolete inventory ratio rose from 25% to 50%. Suppliers got a short
term revenue gain from the inflated sell in volumes, but it was small compared
to the salvage losses and the issue with the return heavy distribution.

**Strategic Implications**
The strategic implications of this case is that supply chain contracts cannot
be considered based on its risk sharing properties. In the literature, buyback
contracts are "superior to wholesale price contracts as a means to coordinate
and achieve benefits for supply chain participants" (pg. 4). However, this case
showed that the buyback contract is still flawed and and can result in the
dysfunctional behaviors. A contract design must take into account the behavior
responses that comes from it. The supply chain contract should also balance
short term revenue and long term supply chain health. The supplier manufacture
volume artificially by pushing excess inventory to make it appear as though
there is revenue growth. In the short term, there is an increase in revenue,
but in the long term, it damages inventory value and the supply chain's health. 
Organizational structures and performance metrics are also considered. The
department that captures revenue will reward volume regardless of downstream
consequences, while the department that bears costs will have no authority to
stop the behaviors that are causing the issue. Organizations must ensure that
performance metrics create shared accountability across commercial and
operational functions. 

**Recommendations and Implementation Considerations**
A Vendor-Managed Inventory system with a base stock policy was proposed as a
solution to the problem. Inventory management authority is transferred to the
supplier instead of the retailer, which removes the retailer's ability to
over order stock and make it so that the dales department cannot inflate
volumes. The base stock policy will make sure that inventory is at a level that
reflects demand and will not have excess. There are some barriers with
implementing this system, which is that retailers must be acclimated to the
change since they will be transitioned away from incentive structures. The
system will also require a real time consumer demand data shared across its
supply chain. There will also be a reduction in sell in volume, which means
stakeholders will have to bear with the short term loss. 
There are some complementary approaches that can be added with this system. A
sell through KPI restructuring is important. Without it, sales incentives will
continue to conflict with the supply chain health. In some organizations, full
VMI system might not be feasible due to retailer bargaining power or
infrastructure gap, in which case advance discount contracts can be the most
efficient for sharing inventory risk.
